Features/Autopilots Experimental

A general contractor for your software.

Put a project on autopilot with a direction, and an agent takes it from there. Like a good contractor, it plans the work, runs a crew of sessions to do it, checks what each one did before it signs off, and comes to you only for the decisions that are yours: which design, which plan, the keys it needs.

It works like a good general contractor.

You don't lay the tiles, and nobody leaves you out either. You say what you want and how far it may go; it runs the job, and brings you the choices that are yours.

You stay the client. The decisions are yours, and those are all it brings you.

You write the brief.

A direction says where to take the project, for whom, and what to leave alone. It's Markdown, and the more it says of why, the better the autopilot decides what you didn't say. Write your own, or start from building a product, keeping it healthy, working through its issues or polishing it.

Then how far it may go. It always starts and steers sessions of its own; you choose what else: merging their pull requests once checks pass, changing the project's settings, setting secrets, adding repositories, setting up schedules. And how hard it works: one to eight sessions at once, a check-in from every 15 minutes to once a day, and the model it thinks with.

  • On your subscription, as you. Putting a project on autopilot takes confirming it's you, as a token that does everything would.
  • Let in by an admin. A workspace's admins let its projects have autopilots first, in its settings.
  • Experimental. It acts on its own and may change: watch its tab, and pause it whenever you like.

It hires the crew.

It writes a plan you can read on its tab, and works through it with sessions of its own, a few at a time: as many at once as you allow, and at most 20 an hour. Each gets a task that says what to build, which files its other open pull requests change, and what may merge without its look. Their pull requests merge once their checks pass, so the next session builds on the last.

Its own session shows every wake and why it woke, what it read, and a live card for each session it starts. Its sessions carry its mark in every list, and a link back to its tab.

Nothing ships until it's checked.

A session that says it's done has said so, not shown it. The autopilot reads its last turn before it takes its word, asks for screenshots of the case that matters rather than the empty one, and pushes back with one concrete thing when a pass finds nothing to fix. Review agents read every commit too, and what they find goes back to the session that wrote it.

farm-portal's autopilot, to farm-portal#8 Your screenshots show an empty list, so they show none of the units. Show a week with six crops filled in, some in crates, some in bunches and some in kilos, at a phone's width, in Swedish. Then merge once checks pass.

It brings you only your decisions.

When only a person can decide, it asks, and gets on with what it can meanwhile. A question comes with up to six answers to click and whatever helps you answer: on Monday it asked Noor which Wednesday reminder farms should get, with the two emails its session drew, side by side.

Whoever follows its session hears of each ask, on their phone too, and finds it waiting in Needs you. An answer wakes it within seconds.

It asks you for the keys.

When the work needs a secret, it asks for it by name and says what for. You type it in on its tab, or with fctl autopilot secret, which doesn't show what you type. It goes into the project's secrets, sealed, and the sessions that start from then on have it. The autopilot hears only that it's set: the value never passes through its conversation.

On Friday evening it asked for RESEND_API_KEY, to email farms their sign-in links, and Tomás gave it the shop's. It replaces and removes only the secrets it set itself.

Walk on site whenever you like.

Leave it a note: a priority, a constraint, something it should know. A note stays until someone removes it, and the newest wins, over older ones and over the direction. It wakes within seconds, and its tab says when it saw each note.

  • Yours to stop. Pause it, resume it, wake it, hand it to a fresh session, or take the project off autopilot.
  • People's sessions stay theirs. It never messages or stops a session someone started.
  • But their pull requests are its concern. Once one looks ready or stuck, it asks whether to help get it merged, and with your yes, a session of its own brings it up to date and merges it.

A long weekend on autopilot.

Noor put Farm portal on autopilot on Friday at 17:40 and went home. This is what it did until Tuesday's breakfast: every session it started, what it asked, and the four times people stepped in.

What wakes it

Between wakes its own session rests, and uses none of your plan. Each wake brings what's new since the last: notes, answers, its sessions that moved, people's pull requests left open.

  • Its check-inAt least this often: every 15 minutes to once a day.
  • A session's turn endsA minute later, with any others that ended meanwhile.
  • You steer or answerFive seconds later, so that answers given in a row go together.
  • A pull request mergesOr closes, for a session of its own that rests.
  • The time it choseWhen it knows when to look again: 5 minutes to a day ahead.
  • You wake itFrom its tab, or with fctl autopilot wake.

It never loses the thread.

It doesn't run in one endless conversation. A fresh session takes over every 12 hours, or once its context grows past the size you choose, and starts from what it wrote down: its plan, its log, and the project's memory, which you read too. Each wake's message says the time and what changed, so that it starts every turn from where things stand rather than from where it left them.

Within the limits you set.

It acts as you, with a maintainer's role at most, in its own project. Its sandbox holds no token: its requests go through its session to fctl, which answers each as anyone's, held to its project and its powers. Its access ends with its session.

It may

  • Always: read its project's sessions, schedules, repositories, memory and the names of its secrets.
  • Always: start sessions, and message, stop, resume or archive the ones it started; keep the project's memory.
  • Merge, once checks pass: its sessions merge their own pull requests.
  • Settings: change the project's settings and its review agents.
  • Secrets: set them, and replace or remove only the ones it set.
  • Repositories and schedules: add them, change them, remove them.
  • Projects, for a workspace's autopilot: create them.

It never

  • Changes people, roles, shares or access tokens.
  • Touches runners, GitHub accounts or the workspace's settings.
  • Deletes a project, or does anything that takes confirming it's you.
  • Messages or stops a session someone started.
  • Starts more sessions at once than you allow, or more than 20 an hour.

Or the whole workspace.

A workspace's admins can put all of it on autopilot, from one of its projects. It starts sessions in any of them, and creates new projects as the work needs them. What it does across the workspace is its admins' to see and steer: everyone else sees that it runs, and its sessions in their projects, with its mark.

From your terminal, too.

Everything the tab does, fctl autopilot does: put a project on autopilot, steer it, answer it, give it the secret it asked for without showing what you type, pause it. In its own sandbox, the autopilot uses the same command to keep its plan and log, and to ask you.
